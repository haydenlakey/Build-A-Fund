## What happened in South Africa

The Asian crisis that began in Thailand in 1997 made foreign investors wary of every emerging market. In South
Africa, Reserve Bank Governor Chris Stals described the swing in September 1998: about **R16 billion of net inflows**
from non-residents in January to April 1998 were followed by about **R19 billion of net outflows** from May to August.

The rand took the strain. Over the four months of pressure it "depreciated by about 25 per cent" (Stals); on the
Federal Reserve's rates it went from about **R5.06 to the dollar in early May to R6.64 on 27 August** (FRED).

Interest rates shot up. The yield on long-term government bonds rose from "just below 13 per cent" in early May to
"over 21 per cent" at its peak, and Stals said borrowing from banks had "become almost prohibitive". The Reserve Bank
also sold a large amount of forward cover (contracts promising foreign currency at a fixed future rate) to residents
and non-residents to discourage money from leaving (Stals).

On the app's data, the **Top 40 index fell 41%** in rand from 1 May to 11 September 1998 (price only), and about
**53%** in US dollars.

## Russia's default

A little more than a month after an international package of emergency financing was announced, on **17 August 1998** Russia was forced to devalue the rouble, announced it would restructure its domestic
government debt falling due by the end of 1999, and imposed a **90-day moratorium** on banks' repayments of foreign
debt. On 2 September the central bank let the rouble float: by 9 September it took **21 roubles** to buy a dollar,
more than three times the **6.29** of 14 August (Kharas, Pinto and Ulatov, 2001).

Russia's default sent investors everywhere rushing to the safest assets, which set off the losses that brought down
the hedge fund LTCM later that year (see the LTCM case file).

## And then it turned

The pressure eased in the last months of 1998. The rand recovered to about R5.50 by early November and ended the year
near R5.90 (FRED).

## Who was hurt

South African borrowers paying sharply higher interest rates; shareholders in JSE companies; holders of long-term
bonds bought before yields jumped; and investors in Russian government debt.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Contagion is real.** A South African fund can be hit by events in Asia and Russia, through the rand, bond yields
  and foreign selling of JSE shares all at once.
- **A weak rand and falling JSE shares can arrive together.** Offshore holdings cushioned the blow for a rand
  investor: from 1 May to 11 September 1998 the S&P 500 fund SPY lost 9% in dollars but gained about 13% in rand
  (app data, using FRED's exchange rate).
- **Interest-rate defence of a currency has costs.** Bond prices fall when yields jump.
