## What happened

After a long boom, the prices of the things the world digs up and pumps out fell hard. The World Bank's
figures for a single year tell the story:

- **Oil prices fell by 47% in 2015**, and **metals by 21%** (World Bank, January 2016).
- By April 2016, **oil and metals prices were 50% to 70% lower than their early-2011 peaks** (World Bank,
  April 2016).

South Africa's stock market is full of mining and energy companies, so the slump hit the JSE directly. On the
app's own price data (weekly closes, in rand, dividends reinvested):

- **Anglo American** fell **81%** from its high in late July 2014 to its low in early January 2016.
- **Glencore** fell **73%** over the same stretch.
- The **Top 40 index** fell only **14%** from its April 2015 high to January 2016. The rest of the market
  (and the weaker rand) held it up.

## Why the miners fell further than the metals

A mining company's costs barely move when prices fall: the mine, the workers and the debt all still have to be
paid. So a 20% fall in the price of what it sells can wipe out far more than 20% of its profit. **Debt** makes
this worse: interest is due whatever the price of copper, so shrinking profits raise the question of whether the
debt can be repaid.

The two biggest JSE-listed miners both had to change course, and both made cutting debt and stopping dividends
part of it:

- **Glencore (September 2015)** announced measures with "an aggregate value of up to US\$10.2 billion" to cut its
  debt, including selling up to **US\$2.5 billion of new shares** and suspending its dividend "until further
  notice", aiming to cut net debt to "the low US\$20s billion by the end of 2016".
- **Anglo American (December 2015)** suspended its dividend for the second half of 2015 and for 2016, planned to
  cut the number of assets it owned by about **60%**, and to reduce its workforce **from 135,000 to 50,000**.

## The rand cushioned the fall, for a rand investor

The rand weakened from about **R10.63 to the dollar** at the end of June 2014 to about **R16.52** in late January
2016 (app data). Because commodities are priced in dollars, a South African holding broad commodities lost much
less in rand than a dollar investor did: the Invesco DBC commodity fund fell about **34%** in rand from its June
2014 high to its April 2016 low (app data). The same weak rand that hurt South Africans' buying power softened
the blow to anything priced in dollars.

## And then it turned

Cycles run both ways. From their January 2016 lows to the end of 2016, Anglo American **rose 253%** and Glencore
**160%** in rand (app data). An investor who sold in the panic of January 2016 locked in the loss and missed the
recovery; one who had owned a large slice of mining shares had to sit through an 80% fall to see it.

## Who was hurt

Shareholders in mining and energy companies, who lost most of their investment on paper and their dividends;
employees, in Anglo American's case through a plan to cut its workforce by more than half; and South African
funds with a heavy resources weighting. A fund tracking the whole JSE carried part of the fall, but spread across
many other sectors.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Resources are cyclical.** Their profits rise and fall with commodity prices, and those cycles last years.
- **Look at how much of your fund depends on one set of prices.** Mining shares, oil companies, platinum and gold
  ETFs and commodity funds can all fall together when commodity prices fall.
- **Debt makes the down-cycle worse.** A company with large debts has the least room when prices fall.
- **The rand can be a partial cushion** for dollar-priced assets, but it doesn't protect a company whose costs are
  in rand and whose selling prices have collapsed.
