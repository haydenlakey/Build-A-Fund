## A new idea: turn government debt into shares

After long wars, both France and Britain had large government debts. In 1719–20 each tried a new solution: a
company would take over the government's debt, giving bondholders **shares in the company** instead, and the
company would be granted valuable trading rights in return. Frehen, Goetzmann and Rouwenhorst describe this as
"converting the national debt into corporate stock" (NBER, 2009).

## Paris: John Law and the Mississippi Company

The Scottish economist **John Law** persuaded the French regent to let him found a bank (nationalised as the Banque
Royale in December 1718) and a trading company to develop the Louisiana territory. Through a series of mergers paid
for by issuing more shares, by July 1719 his **Indies Company** (the "Mississippi Company") held a monopoly on
virtually all of France's overseas trade. It then took over tax collection and offered to refinance the whole
national debt, paying for it with new shares (Velde, 2007).

Shares were sold on instalments, so buyers paid a deposit and owed the rest later, which magnified their gains and
losses. In the street market, the share price went **from 500 livres in May 1719 to 10,000 in late December 1719**.
In January 1720 Law was also made finance minister (Velde).

To hold the share price up, Law pegged it above its market level in bank notes, which meant issuing huge amounts of
paper money in exchange for shares. Inflation followed, and an attempt to control it in **May 1720** "broke the trust
in his System". Share prices collapsed, the notes lost value, and Law left France in **December 1720** (Velde).

## London: the South Sea Company

The **South Sea Company**, founded in 1711, held the exclusive right to supply enslaved Africans to Spanish America.
In 1720 it proposed to take over about **£30 million** of the national debt in exchange for its shares (Temin).

Its share price rose from about **£140 in January 1720** to **£800 at the start of June** and **near £1,000** in the
summer, then "fell precipitously in September, back to £200 by October" (Temin). The **Bubble Act of 9 June 1720**
chartered two new insurance companies, the London Assurance and the Royal Exchange Assurance, and limited the
chartering of competitors (Frehen, Goetzmann and Rouwenhorst).

## More than madness

Using newly collected prices for British and Dutch companies, Frehen, Goetzmann and Rouwenhorst found the bubble
spread across Paris, London and the Netherlands, but not to every share equally. They found "evidence against
indiscriminate irrational exuberance and evidence in favor of speculation about two factors: the Atlantic trade
and the incorporation of insurance companies". Their conclusion: "financial bubbles require a plausible story to
justify investor optimism". When the crash came, though, "September and October were terrible months for all
stocks", whatever story had driven them up.

## Who was hurt

Former government bondholders who had swapped safe(ish) bonds for shares near the top; investors who had bought
shares on instalments and still owed the rest; and, in France, anyone holding Law's bank notes as they lost value.
France spent from 1721 to 1723 sorting out "the paper wreck of the System" (Velde).

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Look for the story behind a price rise.** New trade routes and new kinds of company were real in 1720, as the
  internet was in 1999. The question is whether the price already assumes far more than the story can deliver.
- **Buying with money you will owe later is leverage.** Instalment shares magnified 1720's losses just as margin
  loans did in 1929.
- **Crashes spread.** Markets that rose on different stories still fell together in the autumn of 1720.
