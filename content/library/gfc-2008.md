## What happened

In the early and mid-2000s, American lenders made more and more **high-risk mortgages** and funded them by
bundling the loans into pools and selling them on as securities (Federal Reserve History). From 2001 to 2007,
US mortgage debt per household rose more than 63%, from US\$91,500 to US\$149,500, "even while wages were
essentially stagnant" (Financial Crisis Inquiry Commission, FCIC).

Then US home prices turned. They fell **about 30%, on average, from their mid-2006 peak to mid-2009**. In April
2007 New Century Financial, a leading subprime lender, filed for bankruptcy; in the summer of 2008 the
government took over the two giant mortgage companies, Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac (Federal Reserve History).

The banks that had bought, packaged and held these loans began to fail:

- **Bear Stearns**, an investment bank, avoided bankruptcy only through a government-backed sale to J.P. Morgan
  Chase on 16 March 2008.
- **Lehman Brothers** declared bankruptcy early on **15 September 2008**, with **US\$639 billion** in assets and
  **US\$613 billion** in debts. It had about 25,000 employees, and the government refused to bail it out
  (HISTORY.com).

## Why it spread: borrowing, and borrowing overnight

The FCIC, the US commission that investigated the crisis, put **"excessive borrowing, risky investments, and
lack of transparency"** at its centre. In its words, as of 2007 the five big investment banks (Bear Stearns,
Goldman Sachs, Lehman Brothers, Merrill Lynch and Morgan Stanley) "were operating with extraordinarily thin
capital":

- By one measure their **leverage ratios were as high as 40 to 1**: for every US\$40 of assets, only US\$1 of
  their own capital to absorb losses. **"Less than a 3% drop in asset values could wipe out a firm."**
- Much of the borrowing was **overnight**, so it had to be renewed every day. At the end of 2007 Bear Stearns had
  US\$11.8 billion in equity, US\$383.6 billion in liabilities, and was borrowing as much as US\$70 billion
  overnight.
- Fannie Mae and Freddie Mac's combined leverage, counting the loans they owned and guaranteed, stood at
  **75 to 1** at the end of 2007.

This is the same arithmetic as LTCM's in 1998, on the scale of a whole financial system. When lenders
stop rolling over overnight loans, a borrower has to sell whatever it can, at whatever price it can get, and
those sales push prices down for every other holder of the same assets.

## Markets fell almost everywhere

- The **S&P 500 fell 57%** from its October 2007 peak to its trough in March 2009 (Federal Reserve History).
- The US recession ran from December 2007 to June 2009. Output fell 4.3% and unemployment peaked at
  **10% in October 2009**. Household net worth fell from about US\$69 trillion in 2007 to US\$55 trillion in 2009
  (Federal Reserve History).

South Africa's banks were not at the centre of the crisis, but its share market fell with the rest of the
world. On the app's own price data (weekly closes):

- The **Top 40 index fell 47%** from its high on 16 May 2008 to its low on 21 November 2008, and did not
  get back to its May 2008 level until February 2012 (price index, dividends not included).
- **Bank of America** lost **93%** in dollars from October 2007 to March 2009.
- **FirstRand** fell **51%** and **Standard Bank 46%** from October 2007 to March 2009 (dividends reinvested).

## The rand cushioned the fall, for a rand investor

The rand weakened from about **R6.81 to the dollar** in October 2007 to about **R10.50** in March 2009 (app data).
Over that stretch the S&P 500 fund SPY lost **55% in dollars but only 30% in rand**. As in other global
crises, a weak rand softened losses on offshore holdings for South Africans, although it did nothing for
shares priced and earning in rand.

## Who was hurt

As the FCIC's report went to print in January 2011, more than **26 million Americans** were out of work, could
not find full-time work, or had given up looking. About **four million families** had lost their homes to
foreclosure, and nearly **US\$11 trillion** in household wealth had "vanished, with retirement accounts and life
savings swept away".

## And then it turned

On the app's data, from their 2008–09 lows to the end of 2010 the
Top 40 index rose **77%** (price only) and FirstRand **107%**. An investor who sold near the bottom
locked in the loss; one who held on had to sit through a near-halving to see the recovery.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Leverage is the multiplier.** At 40 to 1, a 3% fall can wipe out the owners. The same logic applies to any
  fund that borrows to invest.
- **Short-term borrowing can disappear overnight.** That is what turns a loss into a forced sale.
- **In a crisis, correlations rise.** Shares in different countries and sectors fell together, so a fund
  spread across many shares still lost heavily. Long-dated US government bonds went the other way: the TLT fund
  rose 26% in dollars from October 2007 to March 2009 (app data).
