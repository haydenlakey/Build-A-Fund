## What happened

On the evening of **Wednesday 9 December 2015** President Jacob Zuma removed Nhlanhla Nene as Minister
of Finance in a terse statement, and appointed David (Des) van Rooyen, a little-known ANC backbencher.
The country had just been downgraded by Fitch to one notch above "junk".

The rand fell sharply to **R15.24 per US dollar** shortly after the announcement, TimesLIVE reported.
In the app's weekly data, the dollar went from **R14.35 to R15.40** between 4 and 11 December, and
**FirstRand, Standard Bank, Absa and Nedbank fell between 15% and 22%** in that one week.

On Sunday 13 December the Presidency announced that Zuma had "received many representations to reconsider"
and appointed Pravin Gordhan, who had held the post before, as Minister of Finance; van Rooyen was moved to
another ministry. Markets partly recovered, but in the app's data the rand kept weakening into January
2016, past R16 per dollar.

## Why banks fell hardest

South African banks earn most of their money inside the country, so their fortunes are tied to the
economy and to confidence in the government. The Top 40 index fell far less (about 2% in that week)
because many of its largest companies earn much of their money abroad, and those holdings gain in rand
when the rand weakens.

## Who was hurt

Anyone whose savings were concentrated in South African banks, government bonds and rand. Investors with
offshore assets or rand-hedge shares saw part of the loss offset, because those holdings rose in rand as
the rand fell.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Home bias concentrates political risk.** A fund made only of SA banks, SA bonds and SA property can
  look diversified across asset classes while depending on the same thing: confidence in South Africa.
- **Currency is a risk and a cushion.** Holdings priced in dollars, or companies earning abroad, tend to
  gain in rand precisely when a local shock weakens the rand.
