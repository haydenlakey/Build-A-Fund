## What happened

**Neil Woodford** was a UK fund manager. His flagship fund, the **Woodford Equity Income Fund (WEIF)**, run by
his firm Woodford Investment Management (WIM), was an open-ended fund: like a South African unit trust,
investors could buy in or cash out on any dealing day at the day's price.

The fund grew to **over £10.1 billion in May 2017**. As its value fell, withdrawals rose (FCA). To pay
investors who left, the fund sold what it could sell easily. According to the FCA, between **July 2018 and June 2019**
WIM and Woodford made "unreasonable and inappropriate investment decisions", **selling more liquid holdings
and buying less liquid ones**. Each withdrawal left the remaining investors owning a larger share of hard-to-sell
assets.

By the run-up to the suspension the fund was down to **£3.6 billion**, and **only 8% of its investments could
be sold within 7 days**.

## The suspension

In **June 2019**, following a wave of new redemptions, dealing in the fund was **suspended**: no one could buy
or sell units. Kent County Council had told the fund it would withdraw its holding, reported by the
*Financial Times* (via interactive investor) at about **£250 million**.

## The consequences

In August 2025 the FCA announced fines of **£40 million** for WIM and **£5,888,800** for Neil Woodford, and a
ban on him managing funds for retail investors. Woodford and WIM referred the decisions to the Upper Tribunal,
so the findings are provisional. Separately, the FCA secured a **£230 million redress scheme** from **Link Fund
Solutions**, the fund's authorised corporate director, which had raised concerns about its liquidity.

## Who was hurt

The investors stuck in the fund, **a significant majority of them ordinary retail customers** (FCA). Investors
who withdrew early were paid from the easiest-to-sell assets; those who stayed were left with the rest.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Liquidity is part of risk.** A holding you can't sell quickly is fine if you never need the money in a
  hurry; it is dangerous when you, or your fellow investors, suddenly do.
- **Check what a fund holds, not only what it promises.** A daily-dealing fund full of unlisted or thinly traded
  shares has a mismatch built in.
- **Star managers are not a substitute for diversification.** A fund's past reputation says little about how it
  will behave when its own investors start to leave.
