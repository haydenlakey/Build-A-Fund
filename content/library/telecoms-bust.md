## What happened

In the late 1990s, telecom companies expected internet traffic to keep growing explosively. They borrowed heavily "to
finance mergers and infrastructure investment", building networks and buying rivals. The projections proved
far too optimistic, leaving what the US Congressional Research Service called "the vast oversupply in
telecommunications capacity". The debt remained (CRS, 2002).

On Yahoo Finance's data (chart), from their 2000 peaks to their 2002 lows:

- **Ericsson's** US-listed shares fell **99%**,
- **Nokia's** fell **82%**, and
- **Verizon**, a large and established phone company, fell **57%** (all with dividends reinvested),
- against **49%** for the S&P 500 index.

The CRS listed Qwest Communications, Global Crossing, Adelphia and Lucent Technologies among the firms whose difficulties
had been "widely reported".

## WorldCom

**WorldCom** was a large US telecom company. Its shares fell from **US\$64.50
in mid-1999** to less than US\$2 by June 2002, and the market value of its shares from about **US\$150 billion** in
January 2000 to under **US\$150 million** by 1 July 2002.

On 25 June 2002 it admitted that it had classified over **US\$3.8 billion** of "line costs" (payments for using other
companies' networks) as **capital expenditures** instead of current expenses. Treating a running cost as an investment
spreads it over future years, so reported profits and assets were both inflated. WorldCom filed for Chapter 11 on
**21 July 2002**, with **US\$103.8 billion** of assets and US\$41 billion of debt: "the largest in U.S. history" at the
time (CRS).

## Who was hurt

Shareholders across the sector; lenders to over-indebted telecom companies; and employees. WorldCom employees'
retirement funds fell from **US\$642.3 million** to less than **US\$18.7 million** (CRS).

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **A booming industry can overbuild.** When every company invests for the same growth, capacity can outrun demand.
- **Debt magnifies a sector bust,** turning a fall in profits into bankruptcies.
- **Watch the accounts in hard times.** Capitalising running costs, as WorldCom did and Tongaat Hulett later did, makes
  profits look better than they are.
