## What happened

For most of 2001 the rand drifted weaker, from about **R7.57 to the dollar** at the start of the year to about
**R8.50** in early September. Then it fell off a cliff: **R9.96** in late November, **R13.60** on 20 December, and
**R12.00** at the year end (Federal Reserve noon rates in New York, via FRED).

The National Treasury's 2002 Budget Review put it this way: the rand **"depreciated by 37,4 and 34,2 per cent in
nominal terms against the US dollar and Euro, respectively, in 2001"**, and "the depreciation of the rand towards
the end of the year was overdone".

## Two ways to measure the same fall

Here is a trap worth knowing. The rand went from R7.57 to R12.00 per dollar, which is **59% more rand per dollar**.
But the rand's value *in dollars* went from 1/7.57 = 13.2 US cents to 1/12.00 = 8.3 cents, a fall of **37%**. Both
describe the same move. In general, if a currency loses a fraction *d* of its value, the other currency rises by
*d* / (1 − *d*), so the two numbers drift further apart the bigger the move.

## Why it fell

The Treasury pointed to several possible reasons, without settling on one:

- reduced appetite for emerging-market assets, partly because of Argentina's debt default;
- speculation against the rand "in comparatively thin currency trading conditions";
- political stresses in Zimbabwe;
- depressed commodity prices; and
- foreign investors selling South African shares and bonds, together with rising dividend payments abroad.

It stressed that "these and other factors ... do not call into question the underlying health of South Africa's
balance of payments". Its table comparing rand crashes shows how unusual 2001 was: inflation was 5.1% and the
budget deficit 1.6% of GDP, against 16.3% and 2.7% in the 1985 crash (2002 Budget Review).

The government appointed the **Myburgh Commission** to investigate whether any transactions or broken rules had
caused the fall. Before its report, the forensic investigators it used had found "no evidence of unethical
behaviour" in the currency market (Deloitte & Touche), which they put down to "the combined impact of the millions
of transactions that make up the market, which reflected an aversion to holding rands", and "no indication" that
foreign banks were responsible (KPMG) (Mail & Guardian).

## What it meant for a South African investor

On the app's data (weekly closes, using the Federal Reserve's exchange rate for 2001–02):

- In **2001**, the US S&P 500 fund SPY **lost 10% in dollars but gained 42% in rand**. The JSE's Top 40 index
  **rose 28% in rand** (price only) but **fell 20% in dollars**.
- In **2002**, as the rand recovered, the effect reversed: SPY **lost 24% in dollars and 44% in rand**, while
  the Top 40 **fell 14% in rand but rose 18% in dollars**.

Imported goods became more expensive too: the Treasury noted the weaker rand pushed up maize and food prices,
and CPIX inflation rose from 5.8% in September to 6.5% in December 2001 (2002 Budget Review).

## And then it turned

By the end of 2002 the rand was back at **R8.59**, and in mid-2003 about **R7.50**, roughly where it started 2001
(FRED). Someone who moved their savings offshore at R12 or R13 to the dollar locked in the weakest rand of the
period and then watched it strengthen.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Currency moves can be huge and fast,** even when the economy itself hasn't changed much.
- **Offshore holdings are a two-way bet on the rand.** They cushion a rand crash and hurt when the rand
  recovers. The app shows everything in rand so you can see this effect in your fund's history.
- **Percentages of currency moves depend on which way you quote them.** Check which one a headline is using.
