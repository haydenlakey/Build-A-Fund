## What happened

On 29 January 2008 Lehman Brothers, a large US investment bank, reported record revenues of nearly **US\$60
billion** and record earnings of more than **US\$4 billion** for 2007. In January 2008 its shares traded as high as
US\$65.73, valuing it at over US\$30 billion. By 12 September 2008 they had closed **under US\$4**, a fall of
nearly 95%. On **15 September 2008** Lehman filed for Chapter 11 protection, "the largest bankruptcy proceeding ever
filed" (Valukas examiner's report).

The court-appointed examiner, Anton Valukas, concluded that "there are many reasons Lehman failed, and the
responsibility is shared", and that Lehman "was more the consequence than the cause of a deteriorating economic
climate". The case file on the 2008 global financial crisis covers that wider crisis; this one looks at two lessons
specific to Lehman.

## Lesson one: leverage can be dressed up

In 2007–08 Lehman knew its **net leverage** (a measure of its assets for each dollar of capital) was critical to rating agencies and to
the firms that lent to it. When it announced a quarterly loss of **US\$2.8 billion** in June 2008, it stressed that
it had cut net leverage to less than 12.5 (Valukas).

What it didn't disclose was **"Repo 105"**. In an ordinary repo, a bank sells assets for cash and agrees to buy them
back days later; the assets stay on its balance sheet. Lehman did the same thing, but because the assets handed
over were worth 105% or more of the cash received, accounting rules let it treat the deal as a **sale**. This
temporarily removed about **US\$50 billion** of assets from its balance sheet at the end of the first and second
quarters of 2008:

- With Repo 105, Lehman reported net leverage of **12.1** at the end of June 2008; with ordinary repos it would have
  been **13.9**.
- Lehman's own accounting staff called it an "accounting gimmick", and its Global Financial Controller said "there
  was no substance to the transactions".
- Lehman did not disclose it to the government, the rating agencies, its investors or its own board. Its auditor,
  Ernst & Young, "were aware of but did not question" it.

The examiner found a "colorable claim" (one strong enough to be argued in court) that its "sole function as
employed by Lehman was balance sheet manipulation" (Valukas).

## Lesson two: the money market fund that broke the buck

Money market funds aim to keep their price at exactly **US\$1 a share** and are treated by many investors as
equivalent to cash. They earn their return by lending short-term to banks and companies, including Lehman.

The **Reserve Primary Fund** held **US\$785 million** of Lehman-issued securities. On 15 September it was unable to
meet investors' requests to withdraw their money, and on **16 September 2008** its value fell below US\$1 a share:
it "broke the buck". The SEC later charged its operators with fraud, alleging they had implied the manager would
support the US\$1 price when it had no such plan, and understated how much investors were asking to withdraw (SEC).

On **19 September 2008** the US Treasury announced it would insure the holdings of eligible money market funds for
a year, backed by up to **US\$50 billion**, because "concerns about the net asset value of money market funds
falling below \$1" had worsened the turmoil (US Treasury).

## Who was hurt

Lehman's shareholders lost nearly everything. Investors in the Reserve Primary Fund couldn't get their "cash" back
when they wanted it. On the app's data, from the end of June to the end of December 2008, Bank of America fell
**31%** and the US financial sector fund XLF **28%**, even measured in rand, which weakened from R7.90 to R9.68 per
dollar. Not every bank fell: JPMorgan Chase ended the half-year **6% higher** in rand (chart).

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **"Cash" funds are loans.** A money market or income fund is only as safe as the companies it lends to, and how
  spread out those loans are.
- **Reported leverage is a number a company chooses how to present.** Figures that improve only on reporting
  dates are a warning sign.
- **A run can hit a fund as well as a bank.** If many investors want their money back at once, a fund may not be
  able to pay all of them at the price they expect.
