## The century view

Every year the London Business School economists Elroy Dimson, Paul Marsh and Mike Staunton publish returns for the
world's share, bond and cash markets back to 1900, now with UBS. The 2026 edition found that **South Africa was the
best-performing share market in the world since 1900** after inflation, with an annualised real return of
**approximately 7%**, ahead of its closest competitors, the US and Australia (as reported by Daily Investor).

Across all markets, the Yearbook's summary reports that world equities returned **6.6% a year after inflation** from 1900
to 2025, against **1.6%** for bonds. It also notes that equities and bonds have "on several occasions lost more than 70%
in real terms", while a 60:40 blend of the two "has never declined more than 50%" (Yearbook 2026 summary).

That record came through wars, the Great Depression, political turmoil and policy uncertainty. It also comes with a
caveat the authors stress: the ranking covers only countries with **uninterrupted** share-market histories. Several
markets that might have rivalled South Africa were closed, nationalised or wiped out by war or revolution, so they
drop out of the comparison (Daily Investor).

## The shorter view, from the app's own data

Shorter periods vary much more:

- From the end of **1995 to the end of 2024**, the Top 40 price index (without dividends) rose about **9.5% a year**,
  against SA inflation of about **5.4%** (app calculation; FRED for inflation). Dividends would add to that.
- From the end of **2009 to the end of 2024**, the **Satrix 40** index fund, with dividends reinvested, returned about
  **7.8% a year**, only about **2.6% a year above inflation** (chart). After inflation, it was worth about the same at
  the end of 2023 as at the end of 2013: a flat decade.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **The long run has favoured shares,** in South Africa more than almost anywhere.
- **Your own holding period is not the long run.** Fifteen years of a Top 40 fund beat inflation by only a small margin.
- **Look at real returns.** A fund that roughly tripled in rand over 2009–24 grew its buying power by under half
  (chart).
- **Survivorship matters.** The countries we have century-long records for are the ones whose markets survived.
