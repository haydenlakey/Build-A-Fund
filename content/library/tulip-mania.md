## The story everyone knows

In 1841 the Scottish writer **Charles Mackay** published *Extraordinary Popular Delusions and the Madness of Crowds*,
which made the Dutch "tulipomania" the world's best-known bubble. In his telling, a single *Semper Augustus* bulb
"was thought to be very cheap at 5500 florins", and one *Viceroy* bulb was paid for with goods worth 2,500 florins:
wheat, rye, four fat oxen, eight fat swine, twelve fat sheep, wine, beer, butter, a thousand pounds of cheese, a bed,
a suit of clothes and a silver cup. When prices collapsed, he wrote, "substantial merchants were reduced almost to
beggary, and many a representative of a noble line saw the fortunes of his house ruined beyond redemption".

## What historians found

The historian **Anne Goldgar** went back to the Dutch archives for her book *Tulipmania* (2007). According to
HISTORY.com's account of her work:

- Mackay relied on **satirical pamphlets and poems** and treated them as fact.
- Prices did spike, from **December 1636 to February 1637**. The most expensive recorded receipts were for **5,000
  guilders**, roughly the price of a nice house, but these were outliers.
- She found only **37 people** who paid more than **300 guilders** for a bulb (about a skilled craftsman's yearly
  earnings), and identified only about **350 people** involved in the trade, probably an undercount.
- She found **no case of anyone going bankrupt** because of the crash. In her words, "the idea that tulip mania
  caused a big depression is completely untrue."

What did happen was a breakdown of trust. Buyers who had promised to pay 100 or 1,000 guilders for bulbs refused to
pay once prices fell, and court records document the defaults. Goldgar calls it a "cultural shock" in an economy
that ran on credit.

The economist **Earl Thompson** (2007) went further: he argued that the reported prices came from contracts that
were in effect converted into **options** (the right, not the obligation, to buy), so they were not prices anyone
had to pay. On that reading "there was nothing maniacal about prices in this period."

## Why it matters

Tulip mania is still the first example most people hear of, and it gets used as shorthand for any price that looks
too high. Looking back, it is easy to call any price that later fell a bubble, a form of hindsight bias. The evidence suggests a short, small episode among a limited group of traders, whose main damage was to
reputations and trust, not to the Dutch economy.

## Who was hurt

Sellers who were promised high prices and never paid, and buyers who were taken to court. Not, on the evidence
Goldgar found, the wider economy.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Check famous stories.** A rule of thumb built on an exaggerated story can mislead you as easily as no rule at
  all.
- **A contract is only as good as the counterparty.** A promised price means little if the buyer won't or can't pay
  when the market turns.
- **Real bubbles leave evidence.** The better-documented cases in this library (1720, 1929, dot-com, Japan) come with
  price records you can check.
