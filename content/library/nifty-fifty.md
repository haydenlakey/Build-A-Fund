## What happened

In the early 1970s, US institutional investors favoured a group of large, fast-growing, famous companies known as
the **Nifty Fifty**, "premier growth stocks, such as Xerox, IBM, Polaroid and Coca-Cola" (Siegel, 1998). The idea
was that these companies were so good that paying a very high price for them was justified.

At the end of 1972 the Nifty Fifty traded at an average **price-earnings ratio (P/E) of 41.9**, more than double the
S&P 500's **18.9**. Polaroid sold for "a fantastic **94.8** times earnings" (Siegel).

## The fall

The 1973–74 bear market "slashed the value of most of the 'Nifty Fifty'", and many investors vowed never again to
pay more than 30 times earnings for a share (Siegel). The whole market fell too: on Yahoo Finance's daily closes the
**S&P 500 fell 48%** from 11 January 1973 to 3 October 1974, and its price didn't regain the 1973 high until July
1980. Over 1973–74, US consumer prices rose about **22%** (FRED), so the fall after inflation was even larger.

## The surprising sequel

In 1998 the economist Jeremy Siegel asked what happened to someone who bought the Nifty Fifty at the 1972 peak and
held on. The answer was more nuanced than "bubble":

- An equally weighted Nifty Fifty portfolio would have returned **12.2% a year** from December 1972 to August 1998
  without rebalancing (12.5% with monthly rebalancing), and "would have nearly matched the S&P 500 over the next 26
  years".
- Some companies were worth even their high 1972 prices. Siegel calculated a **"warranted" P/E**, the price that
  would have given a market-matching return: **68.5** for Philip Morris (investors paid 24), over **82** for
  Coca-Cola and more than **76** for Merck.
- Others were not: IBM, at about 35 times earnings, was worth only **17.1**; Xerox, at **45.8**, only **19.4**.
- Most striking, the **25 with the highest P/Es** (averaging 54) "yielded only about half the subsequent return" of
  the **25 with the lowest P/Es** (averaging 30).

## Who was hurt

Investors who bought the most expensive Nifty Fifty shares near the top, especially those who sold during the
1973–74 fall and so never saw any recovery.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Price matters, even for great companies.** Siegel concluded that growth stocks are not worth buying "at
  any price", though a high P/E alone doesn't rule them out.
- **Waiting decades can rescue an expensive purchase, but not always.** The best Nifty Fifty firms grew into their
  prices; the worst never did.
- **Concentration in a popular theme is a risk.** A fund full of the same favourite growth companies is exposed to
  the same disappointment.
