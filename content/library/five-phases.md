## A pattern, not a law

The economic historian Charles Kindleberger, building on the economist Hyman Minsky, collected
centuries of financial manias in *Manias, Panics and Crashes* (first published 1978) and found the
same story again and again. Authors divide it up slightly differently; this app uses five phases.
Kindleberger himself said the stages are hard to tell apart while you're living through them,
and the pattern describes past bubbles rather than predicting the next one.

## 1. Displacement

Something genuinely new changes what seems possible: a technology (railways, the internet, AI), a
new market opening, or a sharp fall in interest rates. Early investors make real money, and it is
often a sound idea. The trouble comes later.

## 2. Euphoria (boom)

Credit becomes easy and prices rise faster than the profits that are supposed to justify them.
Success stories spread. People who were sceptical start to feel foolish for missing out.

## 3. Mania

Rising prices become the reason to buy. New investors arrive who know little about what they're
buying; borrowing to invest grows; stories of easy riches spread. Valuations stop mattering, and the
phrase "this time is different" appears. New companies or products are created mainly to be sold to
eager buyers.

## 4. Distress

Insiders and well-informed investors quietly sell to lock in gains. Something punctures confidence:
a failure, a fraud, higher interest rates. Prices stall or wobble while optimism gives way to doubt.

## 5. Revulsion (panic)

Everyone heads for the exit at once. Investors who borrowed are forced to sell, which pushes prices
lower still. Prices often fall far below any sensible value, and the asset becomes untouchable for
years. It ends when prices are low enough to tempt buyers back, when trading is halted, or when a
central bank steps in as lender of last resort.

## What it means for a fund builder

- A fund concentrated in whatever has risen most is exposed to phase 5 by construction.
- Holdings that have risen very sharply in a short time deserve a second look at what you are
  paying for: profits, or the hope that someone will pay more later?
- Leverage turns a painful fall into a permanent loss, because forced sellers can't wait for recovery.

The bubble case files in the library (such as the dot-com bubble) show each phase in a real episode.
