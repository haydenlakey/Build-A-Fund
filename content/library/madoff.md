## What happened

Bernard Madoff's firm, Bernard L. Madoff Investment Securities, managed money for investors directly and through
"feeder" funds that placed their own clients' money with him. Its reported returns were remarkable less for their
size than for their smoothness. In 2001 the hedge-fund publication MARHedge reported that Madoff had shown losses "of no more than 55 basis points
in just four of the past 139 consecutive months", with gross returns of "slightly more than 1.5% a month", and
Barron's reported "compound average annual returns of 15% for more than a decade" (both as quoted by the SEC's
Inspector General).

None of it was real. Madoff was paying old investors with new investors' money: a **Ponzi scheme**. It collapsed
when he confessed in December 2008. On 12 March 2009 he pleaded guilty to eleven counts, including securities
fraud, investment adviser fraud and money laundering. On 29 June 2009 he was sentenced to **150 years in
prison** and ordered to forfeit **US\$170.8 billion**, "the total proceeds of and property involved in" certain of
his crimes. The judge called the fraud "staggering" and said "no other white collar case is comparable in terms
of the scope, duration and enormity of the fraud" (US Attorney's Office).

## The red flags

The SEC's Inspector General found that between June 1992 and December 2008 the SEC received **six substantive
complaints** about Madoff, and conducted three examinations and two investigations, but "a thorough and competent
investigation or examination was never performed". Among the warnings:

- **Returns too smooth to be true.** In 2001 the analyst Harry Markopolos showed the SEC that Madoff reported only
  **3 down months** against the S&P 500's **26** over the same period, with a worst month of **−1.44%** against
  the market's **−14.58%**. His conclusion: the numbers "really are too good to be true". His 2005 version, titled
  "The World's Largest Hedge Fund is a Fraud", listed about **30 red flags**.
- **No link to the market.** A respected hedge fund manager complained in 2003 that Madoff's strategy "had no
  correlation to the overall equity markets in over 10 years" and that nobody else could reproduce it.
- **Trading that couldn't have happened.** Madoff claimed to trade far more options than the markets could have
  supported.

## The check nobody made

The Inspector General's expert said the most critical step with a suspected Ponzi scheme is to **verify the
trading through an independent third party**. Madoff told SEC staff in 2006 that his trades cleared through an
account at the Depository Trust Company (DTC) and gave them the account number. He later said he thought "it was
the end game", but the SEC staff never contacted DTC. When the Inspector General did, the records showed one feeder fund's
statement claimed about **US\$2.5 billion** of S&P 100 shares on 31 January 2005, while Madoff's DTC account held
**less than US\$18 million** of them.

## Who was hurt

The scheme "resulted in billions of dollars of losses to thousands of investor-victims" (US Attorney's Office).
The court-appointed trustee has since recovered **US\$15.494 billion** and distributed **US\$14.799 billion** to
customers (Madoff Recovery Initiative, as of 29 September 2026).

## How a statistician would see it

Over 1991 to early 2001 the S&P 500 (chart) rose a lot but zig-zagged: it had falling quarters, and in 1998 and
2000–01 it fell sharply. A record that goes up at nearly the same rate every month has a tiny volatility, so its
**Sharpe ratio** looks extraordinary. That is the clue: high return with almost no risk, sustained for years, is
not something real markets offer. In a fund's statistics, a very high Sharpe ratio is a reason to ask how it was
achieved.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Smoothness is a red flag, not a selling point.** Real assets that earn high returns go down sometimes.
- **Ask who holds the assets.** An independent custodian, administrator and auditor are what separate a real fund
  from a story.
- **Warnings only help if someone follows them up.** The SEC had six detailed complaints and the DTC account
  number, and the scheme still ran until Madoff confessed.
