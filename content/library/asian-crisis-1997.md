## What happened

Before 1997 the economies of East Asia were famous for growth "exceeding 5 percent and often approaching 10
percent" a year. Underneath, according to Federal Reserve History, there were weak spots:

- years of **rapid credit growth** with inadequate supervision of the banks;
- **currency pegs** that had held for a long time, so borrowers assumed the exchange rate wouldn't move;
- **heavy foreign borrowing, often at short maturities**, so loans had to be rolled over frequently; and
- growing **current account deficits** and foreign debt.

On **2 July 1997**, Thailand devalued its currency against the US dollar. Malaysia, the Philippines and Indonesia
soon let their currencies weaken substantially, and foreign lenders pulled back from the whole region (Federal Reserve
History). On the Federal Reserve's exchange rates, the Korean won held steady until October, then collapsed in
November and December.

## How far it went

On the Federal Reserve's exchange rates (via FRED), measured in US dollars from the day before the devaluation to
the low point:

- The **Thai baht** lost **56%** of its value (from 24.52 to 56.1 baht per dollar, January 1998).
- The **Korean won** lost **55%** (from 890 to 1,960 won per dollar, December 1997).
- The **Malaysian ringgit** lost **47%** (from 2.52 to 4.73 ringgit per dollar, January 1998).
- The **Indonesian rupiah** had fallen 30% by early October 1997, and by the end of July 1998 it was about **65%
  below its end-1997 level** (IMF).

Share markets fell too, measured in local currency (Yahoo Finance index closes, from their 1997 highs to their 1998
lows): Korea's KOSPI **−65%**, Indonesia's Jakarta Composite **−65%** and Malaysia's KLCI **−79%**. For a dollar
investor, the currency fall came on top of these losses.

The real economy followed. In 1998 **Indonesia's GDP fell by 14%** and **Korea's by 7%** (IMF). The international
community lent a total of **US\$118 billion** to Thailand, Indonesia and South Korea (Federal Reserve History).

## Why short-term foreign debt is so dangerous

A company that borrows dollars to build something in baht has two risks. If the baht falls, the debt grows in
baht terms while the income doesn't. And if the loan is short-term, lenders can refuse to roll it over, demanding
repayment just when the currency has made it most expensive. When many borrowers face this at once, they all
need dollars, which pushes the currency down further: the same kind of spiral as forced selling in a share market.

## It spread

Trouble in one country made lenders cautious about its neighbours, whatever their own situation. By 1998 other
emerging economies, including **Brazil and Russia**, faced serious balance-of-payments pressure (Federal Reserve
History). South Africa's own currency crisis that year is a separate case file.

## And then it turned

Growth came back in 1999: Korea's economy grew **10.75% in 1999**, Thailand's by over 4%, and
Indonesia returned to small positive growth (IMF). By the end of 1999 the won had recovered to about 78% of its
mid-1997 dollar value and the baht to about 67% (FRED), well above their lows but nowhere near where they started.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Emerging-market holdings carry currency risk on top of share-price risk,** and in 1997–98 the two fell
  together.
- **Countries are connected.** A crisis in one emerging market can hit others, so several emerging-market holdings
  may be less diversified than they look.
- **Debt in a foreign currency is a hidden bet on the exchange rate.** The same applies to a company in your fund
  that earns in rand and borrows in dollars.
