## The thought experiment

The 2008 global financial crisis (see its case file) took the S&P 500 down 57% from its October 2007 peak to March 2009
(Federal Reserve History). On the app's data, the JSE's Top 40 index fell 47% from May to November 2008.

Imagine two South Africans at the start of 2008, each with R24,000 to invest in the Top 40 (price index, before
dividends and costs; app calculation):

- **The lump-sum investor** puts it all in at the end of January 2008. By the end of February 2009 it is worth about
  **R16,400**, a loss of a third. By the end of 2012 it has recovered to about **R34,700**.
- **The monthly investor** puts in **R1,000 at the end of every month** from January 2008 to December 2009, then stops.
  At the worst point, February 2009, the savings are worth about **72%** of what has been paid in. But each R1,000 bought
  more of the index as prices fell, so by the end of 2009 the R24,000 is worth about **R26,800**, and by the end of 2012
  about **R37,500**.

## Why it works

A fixed rand amount buys more units when prices are low and fewer when they are high, so the average cost per unit is
lower than the average price over the period. This is sometimes called **rand-cost averaging**. It doesn't make a crash
painless (the monthly investor was still well behind at the bottom), but the units bought cheaply in late 2008 and early
2009 did most of the work in the recovery.

## The catch

- **It only works if you keep going.** Someone who stopped and sold in early 2009 would have locked in the loss.
- **It isn't magic.** If prices had kept falling for years, as in Japan after 1989, regular buying would have kept
  buying into a falling market.
- **In a rising market, earlier money has longer to grow.** Averaging helps most in falls, and with nerves.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Regular contributions turn a crash into a buying opportunity,** if the money isn't needed and the plan continues.
- **A fund you can't sit through is the wrong fund.** The app's drawdown and stress-test figures show how deep a fall
  your fund might face.
