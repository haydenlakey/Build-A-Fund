## Selling insurance against turmoil

The **VIX** index measures how much volatility options traders expect in the S&P 500 over the next month. It tends to
jump when shares fall. Exchange-traded products let ordinary investors bet on it: "long" products rise when the VIX
rises, and **"inverse"** (short-volatility) products rise when it falls. Betting against volatility is like selling
insurance: you collect a little most days, and pay out heavily in a crisis.

2017 was unusually calm, and these products became popular. Daily open interest in VIX futures grew from about
180,000 contracts in 2011 to **590,000 in 2017**, and leveraged and inverse volatility products held about **US\$4
billion** at the end of 2017 (BIS).

## 5 February 2018

On Monday 5 February the S&P 500 fell about **4%** and the VIX jumped **20 points**, "the largest daily increase in
the VIX since the 1987 stock market crash" (BIS). On Yahoo Finance's closes the VIX more than doubled, from 17.3 to
37.3.

The products' own rules made it worse. To keep their promised exposure, **both** leveraged long and inverse volatility
products had to **buy VIX futures** near the close when the VIX rose. At 16:08, volume spiked to 115,862 VIX futures
contracts within one minute, "roughly a quarter of the entire market", at highly inflated prices. The BIS concluded
that the jump was "largely due to internal dynamics" in these markets, and that synthetic leveraged structures can
"create and amplify market jumps" (BIS).

## XIV: from popular to terminated in a day

Credit Suisse's **XIV** note offered the inverse of short-term VIX futures. The BIS reports that its value "fell 84%
and the product was subsequently terminated". SLCG, a consulting firm, estimated it lost 97% of its value, about
US\$2 billion, on 5 February, and that Credit Suisse announced the next morning it would redeem all the notes. The
note's own prospectus had warned that if the underlying futures rose more than 80% in a day, it was "extremely
likely" the notes would fall to 20% or less of the previous day's value and could be redeemed early (as quoted by
SLCG).

On Yahoo Finance's data, another inverse product, **SVXY**, fell **88%** between the close on Friday 2 February and
the close on Tuesday 6 February.

## Who was hurt

Investors in inverse-volatility products, who had enjoyed steady gains through 2017. SLCG
estimated that buyers who traded XIV after the market close on 5 February, before its true value was published,
paid about US\$700 million more than it was worth.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Steady small gains can hide a large rare loss.** A return series with very low volatility for years may simply
  not have met its bad day yet.
- **Leveraged and inverse products are built for short-term trading,** and their daily rebalancing can force them to
  buy at the top or sell at the bottom.
- **Crowded positions unwind together,** the same lesson as LTCM and the UK pension funds in 2022.
