## What happened

US shares boomed through the late 1920s. Much of the buying was done **on margin**: according to Federal Reserve
History, buyers typically paid about **10%** of a share's price, borrowed the rest, and pledged the shares as
collateral for the loan. The Federal Reserve worried about lending to speculators. The New York Fed wanted to raise
interest rates; the Board in Washington preferred to ask banks not to lend for speculation, and only in August 1929
approved a rise in New York's discount rate to 6%.

The **Dow Jones Industrial Average peaked at 381.17 on 3 September 1929**. Then:

- On **Monday 28 October** the Dow fell **nearly 13%**, and on **Tuesday 29 October** **nearly 12%**.
- By mid-November it had lost almost half its value.
- It kept falling, with rallies along the way, to **41.22 on 8 July 1932**, **89% below its peak**
  (Federal Reserve History).

The broader S&P index (chart) followed the same path: on Yahoo Finance's daily closes it fell **86%** from 16 September
1929 to 1 June 1932.

## Why borrowing made it worse

A buyer with 10% of their own money in a share loses their whole stake if the price falls 10%. When prices fall,
brokers ask for more collateral (a **margin call**); buyers who can't pay are sold out, and their sales push prices
down further, triggering more margin calls. The same mechanism appears in many later case files, from LTCM to the
UK pension funds in 2022.

After the crash the New York Fed bought government securities, lent more through its discount window, cut its
discount rate and assured banks it would supply the reserves they needed. The commercial banks at the centre of
the crash stayed open (Federal Reserve History); the banking panics of 1931–33 came later.

## How long it took to recover

On price alone, the Dow did not regain its 1929 high until **November 1954**, 25 years later (Federal Reserve
History); the S&P index first closed above its 1929 peak in September 1954 (Yahoo Finance).

That overstates the wait for someone who held a broad fund and reinvested dividends, because prices fell during the
1930s too. On Damodaran's annual total-return data, adjusted by the app for US inflation, a dollar in the S&P 500 at
the **end of 1928** was worth less after inflation for eight years and got back above its starting value at the end
of **1936**, before falling back again in 1937 (app calculation; see *A century of US shares*).

## Who was hurt

Above all, those who had bought on margin and were sold out on the way down, losing their own stake and still owing
whatever the sale didn't cover. The banking panics of 1931–33 and the Great Depression followed; Federal Reserve
History treats them as separate events from the crash.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Borrowed money and falling prices don't mix.** Margin buying turned a fall into a collapse.
- **Indexes can fall 80–90%.** Diversifying across many shares didn't protect against a fall in all of them.
- **Dividends and the price level matter for "how long to recover".** A price-only chart can make the wait look longer
  than it was for a reinvesting investor, and changes in the cost of living (US consumer prices fell about 24% from December 1929 to
  December 1932)
  shift it again.
