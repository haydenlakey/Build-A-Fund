## The bet

On 19 December 2007 Warren Buffett made a ten-year bet with Protégé Partners, a firm that invests in
hedge funds. Buffett picked "a virtually cost-free investment in an unmanaged S&P 500 index fund".
Protégé picked five **funds-of-funds**, which between them owned interests in more than 200 hedge funds
run by some of the best-paid professionals in finance. The winner would be whoever earned more, after
fees, from 2008 to 2017. The prize went to charity.

## The result

From Buffett's final scorecard in his 2017 letter:

| | Total gain 2008-2017 | Average yearly gain |
|---|---|---|
| Fund-of-funds A | 21.7% | 2.0% |
| Fund-of-funds B | 42.3% | 3.6% |
| Fund-of-funds C | 87.7% | 6.5% |
| Fund-of-funds D (wound up in 2017) | 2.8% | 0.3% |
| Fund-of-funds E | 27.0% | 2.4% |
| **S&P 500 index fund** | **125.8%** | **8.5%** |

The hedge funds did better in 2008, falling less than the index fund's 37%, but in every one of the
following nine years the funds-of-funds as a whole trailed the index fund.

## Why

Buffett pointed mainly to **fees**: fixed fees averaging about 2.5% of assets a year, paid every year
whatever the results, plus a share of any gains. Over ten years that is an enormous head start to give
away. He also noted that nothing about the decade was unusual: the 8.5% the index delivered was close to
what most experts would have forecast in 2007.

## Two caveats

- This is one bet over one decade in one market. It illustrates a pattern rather than proving a law.
- The index fund is not safe: it fell 37% in its first year. Its advantage came from **staying invested
  and paying almost nothing**, not from avoiding losses.

## The lesson for a fund builder

Every rand paid in fees has to be earned back before you are ahead. Before paying for active
management, ask how much extra return it would have to earn just to break even with a low-cost
index fund.
