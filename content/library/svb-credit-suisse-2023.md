## Silicon Valley Bank

**Silicon Valley Bank (SVB)** served mainly technology and life-sciences companies. Between 2019 and 2021 its group
**tripled in size**, from about US\$71 billion to over US\$211 billion of assets, as venture-capital money poured in
during a period of exceptionally low interest rates. Its deposits were **largely uninsured** (above the government
guarantee limit), and it invested them mostly in **long-term securities** (Federal Reserve review).

When interest rates rose sharply in 2022, those securities fell in value and deposits started flowing out. The Fed's
review found that SVB had breached its own long-term interest-rate risk limits on and off since 2017, changed its
modelling assumptions rather than reduce the risk, and removed hedges that would have protected it against rising
rates.

On **8 March 2023** SVB announced it had sold US\$21 billion of securities at a **US\$1.8 billion** after-tax loss and
would raise capital. The next day it suffered a run: on **9 March** it lost **over US\$40 billion** of deposits, and
management expected **over US\$100 billion** more on 10 March, together roughly **85%** of its deposit base. Its
depositors, a concentrated network of venture-capital investors and tech firms, "fueled by social media, withdrew
uninsured deposits in a coordinated manner at an unprecedented rate". SVB failed on **10 March 2023** (Federal Reserve
review).

The Fed's Vice Chair for Supervision concluded that SVB's board and management "failed to manage their risks", and that
supervisors "did not fully appreciate the extent of the vulnerabilities"; at failure the bank had **31 unaddressed**
supervisory warnings (Federal Reserve).

## Credit Suisse

The Swiss bank Credit Suisse was already suffering a crisis of confidence, with large outflows
of client money; the US bank turmoil made it worse. FINMA, the Swiss regulator, said "there was a risk of the bank
becoming illiquid, even if it remained solvent". On **19 March 2023** it approved a takeover by **UBS**, supported by
central-bank liquidity and a government guarantee. The support triggered "a complete write-down of the nominal value of
all **AT1** debt of Credit Suisse in the amount of around **CHF 16 billion**" (FINMA). AT1 bonds are designed to absorb
losses in a crisis, and their holders were wiped out.

## What the market showed

On Yahoo Finance's data (chart), an ETF of US regional bank shares fell from 91.6 on 8 March to about **58** by early
May (1 February = 100), while the S&P 500 dipped only briefly and was above its February level by the end of May.

## Who was hurt

SVB's and Credit Suisse's shareholders; Credit Suisse's AT1 bondholders; and shareholders in other regional banks. SVB's
uninsured depositors faced the possibility of losing money above the insured limit.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Interest-rate risk hides in "safe" bonds.** Long-term bonds bought when rates are low lose value when rates rise, as
  2022 showed.
- **Runs are faster now.** SVB lost over US\$40 billion in a day; in 2008 Washington Mutual's run took 16 days to
  reach US\$19 billion (Federal Reserve review).
- **Know where you sit in the capital structure.** AT1 bonds sound like debt but can be written down to zero.
