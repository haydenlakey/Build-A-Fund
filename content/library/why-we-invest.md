## What a share is

A **share** is a small piece of ownership in a company. It gives you a claim on the company's
future profits, paid out as dividends or kept and reinvested to grow the business, and usually a
vote on how the company is run. A share price is what buyers and sellers agree that claim is worth
today, which is why it moves when expectations about future profits change.

## Where shares came from

Before the 1600s, merchants mostly pooled money for a single trading voyage and settled up when
the ship came back. On **20 March 1602** the Dutch East India Company (the VOC) announced what
Euronews describes as the first initial public offering. Two things were new:

- **Anyone could invest.** Article 10 of its charter said: "All the residents of these lands may
  buy shares in this Company." There was no minimum. According to Euronews, 3 674 945 guilders were
  raised from 1 143 initial investors, among them a maid, Neeltgen Cornelis, who put in 100 guilders.
- **Shares could be sold to someone else.** Transfers were recorded by the company's bookkeeper,
  so an owner who wanted out could sell rather than wait for the company to end. Shares changed
  hands on Amsterdam's New Bridge, in the Hendrick de Keyser Exchange and, after hours, on Dam Square.

Patience was needed: the first dividend came in August 1609, seven years later, and it was paid
in mace (a spice the company struggled to sell) rather than cash.

## Why shares have tended to grow over time

A company's profits are measured in money. If prices in the economy rise, a business that can
raise its own prices may see its profits rise in money terms too, which is why shares are often
described as a long-run hedge against inflation. "Long-run" matters: in bursts of high inflation
shares have often fallen at first (see the 1970s and 2022 case files when they're added).

## What each building block does

| Asset | What you own | Its usual job in a fund |
|---|---|---|
| **Shares** (equities) | Part of companies | Long-run growth; the most volatile |
| **Bonds** | A loan to a government or company, paid back with interest | Steadier income; can cushion share falls, but not always |
| **Listed property** | Shares in companies owning buildings, paid from rents | Income that can grow with rents; behaves partly like shares |
| **Cash** | Money-market deposits | Stability and something to spend in a crisis; little growth after inflation |
| **Offshore assets** | Shares or bonds outside South Africa | Spread beyond one economy and one currency; gain in rand when the rand weakens |
| **Gold and commodities** | Physical metal or futures | No income; sometimes rises when shares fall; priced in dollars |

None of these is "safe" in every period. Building a fund is about combining them so that no single
thing going wrong can sink it, at a cost you can live with.
