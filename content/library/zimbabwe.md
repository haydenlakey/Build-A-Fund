## What happened

Zimbabwe's economy shrank by about **40%** over 2000–07 and by another **14%** in 2008 (IMF). To pay for spending it
couldn't fund through taxes, the government relied on the **Reserve Bank of Zimbabwe**, which ran "quasi-fiscal
activities" (government-style spending done by the central bank) estimated at **US\$1.1 billion, or 36% of GDP, in
2008**: election-related expenses, subsidised loans and subsidised inputs for farmers. These were paid for largely by
creating money (IMF).

When a central bank creates money far faster than the economy produces goods, prices rise; when people expect them to
keep rising, they spend money as fast as they can, and prices rise faster still. Zimbabwe's official 12-month
consumer price inflation peaked in **September 2008 at almost 500 billion percent** (IMF).

## The end of the Zimbabwe dollar

From **October–November 2008** the Zimbabwe dollar "virtually disappeared from circulation". In **early 2009** the
authorities formalised what people were already doing: they adopted the **rand as the reference currency** and allowed
transactions in hard currencies, and most taxes became payable in foreign currency. Hyperinflation stopped with the end
of the Zimbabwe dollar; in January–February 2009 prices measured in US dollars actually fell (IMF).

## Who was hurt

Almost everyone. Bank deposits fell from almost **US\$1 billion** at the end of 2005 to about **US\$300 million** at
the end of 2008. Electricity, water and rail services nearly collapsed, **70%** of the population needed food
assistance, and a cholera epidemic killed more than **4,000** people (IMF). Savings held in Zimbabwe dollars lost almost all their
value.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Inflation at its extreme destroys money savings.** Foreign currencies kept their value, which is why
  Zimbabweans switched to them before the government made it official.
- **Watch how a government pays its bills.** Money printing to cover spending is the classic route to high
  inflation.
- **Currency diversification is insurance.** A South African fund held entirely in rand depends on one currency;
  offshore holdings spread that risk.
