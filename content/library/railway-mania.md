## What happened

The mathematician and historian Andrew Odlyzko calls the British Railway Mania of the 1840s "by many measures the
greatest technology mania in history". Railways were the new technology of the age, and after a smaller boom in the
1830s, which had worked out well for its investors, enthusiasm returned in the mid-1840s.

Each new line needed an Act of Parliament, so the miles of railway Parliament approved show how fast the mania grew:

- **91 miles** in 1843,
- **805 miles** in 1844,
- **2,700 miles** in 1845, and
- **4,538 miles** in 1846, a record.

Almost all of the money came from private individuals, most of them new to share markets, including Charles Darwin,
John Stuart Mill, the Brontë sisters and leading politicians. At its peak in 1847, railway investment was about
**£44 million a year**, against about £50 million for the whole national government budget (Odlyzko).

## The slow crash

Unlike 1929 or 1987, the fall was slow. Railway share prices peaked in **July 1845** and then fell
"with only a few brief and minor rebounds" to **October 1849**: an index of railway shares went from **167.9 to
60.5**, a fall of about **64%**. In 1849 dividends were cut and the "creative accounting" of George Hudson, the
"Railway King", came to light (Odlyzko).

Shareholders had a further problem: shares were sold **partly paid**. A typical Mania share of £20 needed a deposit
of just **£1**; the other £19 was called from shareholders in stages as construction went on. Investors had to keep
paying these "calls" even as share prices fell, and the pressure of calls was blamed for part of the decline
(Odlyzko).

At the end of 1849 Charlotte Brontë wrote that her shares in the York and North Midland Railway, originally priced
at £50, had "at one time ... rose to 120", and "are now down at 20, and it is doubtful whether any dividend will be
declared this half-year" (quoted by Odlyzko).

## Why the profits never came

Promoters relied on "traffic takers", specialists who forecast how much traffic a new line would carry. Their
forecasts for the railways of the 1830s had turned out to be remarkably accurate, so investors trusted them again.
This time they were far too optimistic. Odlyzko argues the failure was predictable, because applying the traffic
takers' models to all the proposed railways together "produced impossible answers".

The later lines did worst. A shareholders' committee that investigated the York and North Midland Railway found that
its lines authorised before 1845 were earning **7.65%**, those authorised in 1845 **4.15%**, and those authorised in
1846 just **0.07%** (Odlyzko).

In 1855 *The Economist* summed it up: "Mechanically or scientifically, the railways ... are an honour to our age and
country: commercially, they are great failures" (quoted by Odlyzko).

## Who was hurt

The thousands of middle-class and wealthy British families who had put their savings into railway shares. In
Charlotte Brontë's words, "many – very many are – by the late strange Railway System deprived almost of their daily
bread".

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **A great technology is not the same as a great investment.** Britain got a national rail network; the investors
  who paid for it mostly lost.
- **Add up the forecasts.** If every company in a booming sector expects to win the same customers, the forecasts
  can't all come true.
- **Bubbles can deflate slowly.** A four-year slide with small rebounds can tempt investors to keep buying the dip.
- **Partly-paid investments are a form of leverage.** Owing future payments on something that is falling in value
  makes the losses worse.
