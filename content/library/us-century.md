## The numbers

The NYU finance professor Aswath Damodaran publishes the annual returns of US shares, bonds and bills back to 1928.
The app compounded them and took out US inflation (all figures below are the app's calculation from his data and
the US consumer price index):

| 1928–2025 (98 years) | Return a year | After inflation | $100 at the end of 1927 became, after inflation |
|---|---|---|---|
| US shares (S&P 500, dividends reinvested) | 10.0% | 6.8% | about $61,800 |
| US 10-year government bonds | 4.5% | 1.5% | about $412 |
| US Treasury bills (cash) | 3.4% | 0.3% | about $138 |
| Inflation | 3.0% | | |

A difference of a few percentage points a year, compounded for a century, is the difference between multiplying
your money's buying power about 600 times and barely keeping it.

## The price of that return

The average year was good, but almost no year was average:

- The yearly returns of US shares had an average of **11.9%** and a standard deviation of **19.4%**.
- Shares **fell in 26 of the 98 years**. The worst was **1931, −43.8%**.
- After inflation, a dollar in shares at the end of 1928 had lost about **55%** by the end of 1931 (on year-end
  values).
- After inflation, there were long stretches below an earlier peak: **14 years** from the end of 1999 to 2013,
  **12 years** from 1972 to 1984, and 9 years from 1936 to 1945.
- Over **10-year** windows, the real return was negative in about **12%** of cases (worst: 1999–2008, about −3.8% a
  year). Over every **20-year** window in the data it was positive, though barely so for the one starting in
  1929 (0.6% a year).

## Why this is a "counterexample"

Most case files in this library are about things going wrong. This one shows what patience has been paid in the
largest share market: someone who kept a broad, cheap, diversified holding through the Depression, the 1970s, the
dot-com bust and 2008 ended up far ahead of someone who kept their money in "safe" cash.

## The caveats

- **Survivorship.** The US is the market everyone studies because it did so well. Japan's market took 34 years to
  regain its 1989 peak (see the Japan case file), so one country's century is not a law of nature.
- **No costs or taxes.** These are index returns. Fees and taxes reduce them every year, and compounding works on
  costs too (see the Fees article).
- **Dollars, not rand.** A South African investor's experience depends on the rand as well; the app shows your own
  fund in rand.
- **Estimation error.** Even 98 years of yearly returns pins down the average return only roughly: with a standard
  deviation of 19.4%, the standard error of the mean is about 19.4% / √98 ≈ 2.0 percentage points.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Time is what turns volatility into return.** Over one year, shares are a gamble; over twenty, in this history,
  they have always beaten inflation.
- **Cash is safe from crashes, not from inflation.** Treasury bills barely kept up over the century.
- **The ride is part of the deal.** A fund that can't sit through a 50% fall or a decade going nowhere won't
  collect the long-run return.
