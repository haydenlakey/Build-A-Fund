## What happened

The Federal Reserve's own history calls it **the Great Inflation**, "lasting from 1965 to 1982". In 1964 US inflation
was about **1%** and unemployment 5%. By the summer of 1980 inflation was **near 14.5%** and unemployment over 7.5%,
the combination of high prices and weak growth that became known as **stagflation** (Bryan, Federal Reserve History).

According to the essay, the main source was monetary policy that allowed the money supply to grow too fast, partly
because policymakers believed they could permanently trade a little more inflation for lower unemployment. Other
shocks piled on:

- In the summer of **1971** President Nixon stopped foreign central banks from converting dollars into gold, ending
  the dollar's link to gold.
- **Wage and price controls** between 1971 and 1974 slowed price rises only temporarily and worsened shortages.
- The **Arab oil embargo** that began in October **1973** quadrupled the price of crude oil, and the 1979 crisis
  around the Iranian revolution **tripled** it again.

There were four recessions in the period, the last and worst running from July 1981 to November 1982, with
unemployment peaking at nearly 11%.

## What it did to investments

On Damodaran's annual returns, adjusted by the app for US inflation (chart):

- Over **1966–1981**, US shares with dividends reinvested lost about **15%** of their buying power, 10-year government
  bonds about **40%**, and Treasury bills (cash) about **3%**.
- In **1973–74** alone, shares lost about **48%** after inflation (see the Nifty Fifty case file).

Bonds suffered most because their payments are fixed in dollars: when inflation and interest rates rise, those fixed
payments buy less, and new bonds pay more, so existing bonds fall in price. Cash suffered least because its interest
rate kept being reset as rates rose.

## And then it turned

Once inflation was brought down in the early 1980s (see the Volcker shock case file), the same assets recovered
strongly: over 1982–86 shares and 10-year bonds each more than doubled after inflation (app calculation from
Damodaran's data).

## Who was hurt

Savers holding long-term bonds, people living on fixed incomes, and shareholders who needed to sell during the long
flat stretch. Borrowers with fixed-rate debts, by contrast, repaid them in money worth less.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Look at returns after inflation.** A fund can rise in rand while losing buying power.
- **Fixed-rate bonds carry inflation risk;** inflation-linked bonds and short-term cash carry less of it, at the cost
  of lower expected returns.
- **Shares are not an instant inflation hedge.** Over 16 years of high inflation they lost value in real terms, though
  far less than bonds.
