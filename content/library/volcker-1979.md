## What happened

When **Paul Volcker** became chairman of the Federal Reserve in August 1979, US inflation was "above 11 percent"
(Bryan, Federal Reserve History). On **6 October 1979** he announced that the Fed would focus on managing bank
reserves instead of the day-to-day federal funds rate, and warned that the rate "is apt to fluctuate over a wider
range than had been the practice in recent years" (Medley, Federal Reserve History).

It did. The **federal funds rate reached a record 20% in late 1980**. The economy went into "a severe recession", and
unemployment peaked at **10.8% in late 1982** (Medley). Inflation came down: by the end of the 1981–82 recession it was
"back under 5 percent" (Bryan). Different measures give different peaks: the app calculates 14.8% on the US consumer
price index over the 12 months to March 1980, while Medley's essay quotes a peak of 11.6% in March 1980.

## What it did to investments

On FRED's monthly averages (chart), the yield on 10-year US government bonds peaked at **15.3% in September 1981**.
Rising yields meant falling bond prices, so bondholders lost in the run-up. But someone who bought a 10-year bond near
the peak locked in that yield as inflation and rates fell: on Damodaran's data, 10-year government bonds returned
**32.8% in 1982** alone, and over 1982–86 both shares and bonds more than doubled after inflation (app calculation).

## Who was hurt

Borrowers facing very high interest rates, workers who lost jobs in the recession, and holders of long-term bonds bought
before rates peaked. Many people wrote to Volcker saying high rates had left them unable to buy homes they had saved for
(Medley).

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Interest rates can go much higher than recent experience suggests.** Long-term bonds are very sensitive to that
  risk.
- **Pain now can set up gains later.** The highest yields came near the end of the inflation, not the start.
- **Bonds and shares can both fall when rates jump,** as they did again in 2022.
