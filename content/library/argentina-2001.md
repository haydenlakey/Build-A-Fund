## A peg that ended hyperinflation

In early 1991 Argentina's inflation was raging at **27% a month**. Its **Convertibility Plan** fixed the peso at
**one US dollar**, backed by the central bank's reserves. It worked: inflation fell to single digits by 1993, and
growth averaged nearly **6% a year** over 1991–98 (IMF Independent Evaluation Office, IEO).

## Why it broke

The IEO describes vulnerabilities that the good years masked:

- **Government debt kept rising**, much of it in foreign currency and held abroad.
- **The peg ruled out a cheaper peso** just when one was needed: the dollar was strong, and Brazil devalued its
  currency in early 1999. Argentina could only become more competitive through falling prices and wages, so
  "deflation and output contraction set in".
- **The economy was heavily dollarised,** so leaving the peg would be very costly for anyone with dollar debts.

The IMF lent heavily to support the peg. The IEO concluded that Argentine policymakers failed to correct course early
enough, and that the IMF "erred in the precrisis period by supporting the country's weak policies too long".

## The collapse

In 2001, with the economy in its third year of recession, investors pulled out and depositors ran on the banks. On
**1 December 2001** the government introduced a partial **deposit freeze** (the *corralito*) and capital controls.
At the end of December, after President Fernando De la Rúa resigned, Argentina partially **defaulted** on its debt,
and in early **January 2002** it abandoned the peg (IEO).

By the end of 2002 the peso was trading at **3.4 to the dollar**. The economy shrank **11% in 2002**, bringing the fall
since 1998 to **nearly 20%**; unemployment rose above **20%** and poverty "worsened dramatically" (IEO).

## What it did to share investors

On Yahoo Finance's data (chart), Argentina's Merval share index fell from 569 in March 2000 to 244 in September 2001.
In pesos, it then rose in 2002 as the peso collapsed, but measured in dollars it fell further: at the end of June 2002
it was worth about **92** in dollar terms, against 551 at the end of 1999.

## Who was hurt

Argentine savers whose bank deposits were frozen, workers who lost their jobs,
holders of Argentine government bonds, and foreign investors in Argentine shares.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **A fixed exchange rate is a promise, not a law of nature.** When it breaks, years of adjustment can happen in weeks.
- **Measure foreign holdings in your own currency.** In pesos the Merval recovered in 2002; in dollars (or rand) the
  losses deepened.
- **Bank deposits are not risk-free in a crisis.** They can be frozen as well as devalued.
