## What happened

**Enron** began in the regulated business of piping natural gas, then moved into **unregulated energy trading**,
on the idea that more money could be made trading financial contracts tied to energy than from owning pipelines
and power plants. Its revenue grew from under US\$10 billion in the early 1990s to **US\$101 billion in 2000**,
ranking it **seventh on the Fortune 500** (Congressional Research Service, CRS).

In 2001 it unravelled quickly:

- **16 October 2001:** Enron reported a quarterly loss of US\$618 million after a US\$1 billion charge for poorly
  performing businesses.
- **8 November 2001:** it restated its profits since 1997, **reducing them by US\$586 million**.
- **2 December 2001:** it filed for Chapter 11 bankruptcy.

Its shares, which traded for **more than US\$80** in January 2001, were worth **less than 70 cents** in January
2002. Shareholders "lost tens of billions of dollars" (CRS).

## How it hid the problems

Enron set up partnerships, known as **special purpose entities**; for some of them, its board waived its
conflict-of-interest rules in 1999. Under the accounting rules of the time, a company didn't have
to include such a partnership in its own accounts if an outside investor put up **as little as 3%** of its
capital. Enron's partnerships "appear to have concealed debts and liabilities" that would have significantly
changed its reported profits (CRS).

The US Department of Justice later described the scheme as "designed to make it appear that Enron was growing at a
healthy and predictable rate". Enron's auditor, **Arthur Andersen**, "admitted some mistakes" and fired the
partner in charge of the Enron audits in January 2002; Enron dismissed Andersen two days later (CRS).

## What happened to the people in charge

On 25 May 2006 a jury convicted former chief executive **Jeffrey Skilling** of conspiracy, securities fraud and
other charges; he was sentenced to **24 years and four months** in prison and ordered to forfeit about US\$45
million towards restitution for the victims. Founder **Kenneth Lay** was also convicted, but died on 5 July 2006,
and his conviction was voided (US Department of Justice).

## Who was hurt

"Thousands of workers lost their jobs" (US Department of Justice). Many also lost their savings: at the end of
2000, **62% of the assets in Enron's 401(k) retirement plan were Enron shares** (CRS). When the company failed,
those employees lost their income and most of their retirement savings at the same moment.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **One company can go to zero,** however large and admired it is. Enron was one of the biggest companies in
  America less than a year before it failed.
- **Diversification is the defence you control.** You can't audit a company yourself, but you can limit how much
  of your fund depends on any one of them. The app's "largest company" measure looks through ETFs and funds to
  find your biggest single exposure.
- **A job is an exposure too.** Enron's employees were concentrated twice over: their salaries and their
  savings depended on the same company.
