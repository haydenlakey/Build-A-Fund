## What happened

In September 1985 the major economies agreed, in the **Plaza Agreement**, to intervene in currency markets to
correct "the excessive appreciation of the U.S. dollar". The yen rose sharply, and to counter the slowdown that followed, the Bank of Japan cut its official discount rate five
times, by 2.5 percentage points in all, between January 1986 and February 1987. The rate then stayed at
**2.5%**, at the time its lowest ever, for about two years and three months, until May 1989 (Okina, Shirakawa
and Shiratsuka, Bank of Japan, 2001).

Cheap money, rising profits and growing confidence fed on each other. The Bank of Japan economists describe a
process of **"intensified bullish expectations"**:

- The **Nikkei 225** share index peaked at **38,915 at the end of 1989**, **3.1 times** its level at the Plaza
  Agreement (12,598).
- **Urban land prices** peaked in September 1990, almost **four times** their level of September 1985.
- The combined gains on shares and land in 1986–89 came to **452% of Japan's annual GDP**, against 193% in the
  previous boom of 1972–73.

Rising land and share prices also raised the value of the collateral that companies could borrow against, so
borrowing and prices pushed each other up.

## The bust

The Bank of Japan began raising rates on **31 May 1989** (2.5% to 3.25%) and kept going, to 6.0% by August 1990.
Banks became more cautious about lending from late 1989, and much more so in 1990. Then:

- Shares fell to **14,309 in August 1992**, more than **60% below the peak**.
- Losses on shares and land in 1990–93 came to **159% of GDP**.
- By 1999 land prices were about **80% below their 1990 peak**, and about 20% below where they had been in 1985.
- Major Japanese banks' nonperforming (bad) assets were **¥20.3 trillion (4.1% of GDP)** at the end of March 1999,
  or ¥44.6 trillion (9.0% of GDP) counting the loans already written off since 1992 (Okina, Shirakawa and
  Shiratsuka).

## The longest wait

On Yahoo Finance's daily closes, the Nikkei 225 kept falling long after 1992: it hit **7,055 on 10 March 2009**,
**82% below** its 1989 peak. It first closed above the 1989 peak on **22 February 2024**, more than **34 years**
later. These are price-index figures: an investor who reinvested dividends did somewhat better, but still waited
decades.

## Who was hurt

Japanese savers, companies and banks that had bought shares and land near the top, and the banks that had lent
against land as collateral. The Bank of Japan paper notes that the fluctuation in economic growth between the
bubble years (1986–90) and the years after it burst (1991–95) was the largest among the G-7 countries.

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **A whole country can be a bubble,** not just one sector or company.
- **"Long run" can be very long for one market.** The Nikkei needed 34 years to get back to its peak, and a
  single sector or company may never get back.
- **Look at where your fund's money is.** The app's country and region look-through shows how much of your fund
  depends on one economy, including your own.
- **Cheap borrowing and rising collateral values feed each other on the way up and on the way down.**
