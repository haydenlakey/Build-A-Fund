## Fees compound too

A fee is charged on your whole balance every year, including on the growth that earlier years
produced. So its cost compounds just like returns do.

The U.S. SEC illustrates this with $100 000 invested for 20 years at 4% a year before fees:

| Annual fee | Value after 20 years (approx.) |
|---|---|
| 0.25% | $208 000 |
| 0.50% | $198 000 |
| 1.00% | $179 000 |

The difference between a 0.25% and a 1% fee is about $29 000, more than a quarter of the
original investment.

## What South African funds report

Funds here report a **TER** (total expense ratio: management, admin, audit and similar costs)
and **transaction costs** (the cost of the fund's own trading). Their sum is the **TIC** (total
investment charge). Satrix 40, a low-cost JSE index ETF, reported a 0.10% TER and 0.03%
transaction costs on its 31 August 2026 fact sheet. Your platform and brokerage fees come on top.

## Fees against skill

Warren Buffett's ten-year bet (2008-2017) pitted an S&P 500 index fund against five
funds-of-hedge-funds. The index fund gained 8.5% a year; the funds-of-funds between 0.3% and 6.5%.
Buffett pointed to fixed fees averaging about 2.5% of assets a year on the hedge-fund side, paid
whether or not the funds did well. See the *Buffett's bet* case file.

## Show me the maths

With gross return $r$ and annual fee $f$, after $n$ years each rand becomes roughly $(1 + r - f)^n$
(funds deduct fees daily, so the exact figure differs slightly). The share of your final value lost
to fees is about $1 - \left(\frac{1+r-f}{1+r}\right)^n$. With $r = 4\%$, $f = 1\%$ and $n = 20$, that is
about 18%.
