## What LDI is

Many UK companies have promised their employees pensions of a set amount (a **defined benefit**, or DB,
pension). The value of those promises rises when long-term interest rates fall, so DB pension funds hold long-term
government bonds (**gilts**), especially inflation-linked ones, whose prices move the same way. This is called
**liability-driven investment (LDI)**, and over **£1 trillion** was invested this way in the UK (Bank of England).

To hold enough gilts while also owning higher-returning shares, many pension funds **borrowed**, using repo
loans secured on the gilts. LDI funds kept a cushion of capital to absorb losses; if gilt prices fell far enough,
the pension fund had to top up the cushion or the LDI fund had to sell gilts. In the Bank of England's
illustration, a fund at **2 times leverage** sees a 30% fall in gilt prices "severely erode" its cushion
(Bank of England).

## What happened

On **Friday 23 September 2022** the UK government announced its "growth plan". That day sterling fell about 4%
against the dollar and long-term gilt yields rose 30 basis points. On Monday 26 September the 30-year gilt yield
rose about 50 basis points more. Before that week, the biggest one-day rise in the 30-year yield since 2000 had
been 29 basis points; that week there were two daily rises of more than 35 (Bank of England letter).

As gilt prices fell, LDI funds faced **margin calls**. On the evening of Monday 26 September, LDI managers warned the
Bank that they might have to sell **at least £50 billion** of long-term gilts in a short space of time, against
normal trading of about £12 billion a day. On Tuesday evening several managers told the Bank that multiple LDI funds
were likely to fall to a **negative net asset value** and might have to begin winding up the next morning. The banks
that had lent to them would then have sold the gilts held as collateral, "driving a potentially self-reinforcing
spiral" (Bank of England letter).

By 28 September the 30-year yield had risen **130 basis points in three days**, "three times larger" than any other
historical move over a similar period (Bank of England Quarterly Bulletin).

## The rescue

On **Wednesday 28 September** the Bank of England announced it would buy long-dated gilts every weekday until
14 October, to give LDI funds time to rebuild their cushions. The 30-year yield fell more than 100 basis points that
day. In total the Bank bought **£19.3 billion** of gilts (£12.1 billion conventional, £7.2 billion index-linked),
and sold them back to the market between 29 November 2022 and 12 January 2023 (Bank of England).

On Yahoo Finance's prices, an ETF of gilts with more than 15 years to maturity fell **25%** between 1 and 27 September
2022, and an index-linked gilt ETF **28%**, while the FTSE 100 share index ETF fell only about 3.5%. On 28 September
the long-gilt ETF rose about 17%.

## Who was hurt

The Bank noted that a pension fund using LDI might even be better off overall when yields rise, because the value of
its pension promises falls by more than its assets. The danger was **liquidity**, not long-run solvency: the cash
had to be found immediately, by selling assets into a falling, illiquid market. Pooled LDI funds, shared by many
small and medium-sized pension schemes, found it harder to raise more money quickly (Bank of England letter).

## The lesson for a fund builder

- **Leverage creates the need to sell at the worst time,** even on assets that are safe if held to maturity.
- **"Low risk" depends on the time horizon.** Long-dated government bonds are very sensitive to interest rates,
  and their prices can fall a quarter in weeks.
- **Crowded trades unwind together.** When many investors hold the same leveraged position, one investor's forced
  sale lowers prices for all the others.
